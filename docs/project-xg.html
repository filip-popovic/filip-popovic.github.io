Estimating Expected Goals

Can a simple, interpretable logistic regression keep up with machine learning and an industry model when estimating the probability that a shot becomes a goal? Using every shot from the FIFA World Cups 2018 and 2022, this thesis shows that it can.

The question

Expected goals (xG) is one of the central metrics in modern football analytics: it assigns every shot a probability of being scored, based on where and how it was taken. Commercial providers like StatsBomb build their xG models on large proprietary datasets. This thesis asks how close you can get with publicly available data and standard statistical methods, and whether a flexible machine learning model actually beats a classical one.

Data

  • Source: StatsBomb Open Data, all shots from the FIFA World Cups 2018 and 2022
  • Sample: 3,067 shots after excluding penalties, of which 287 were goals (9.35%), a strongly imbalanced target
  • Features: 11 variables, including distance and angle to goal, the goalkeeper's position, defenders in the shooting cone, body part, technique, shot type, pressure, first-time shots and one-on-ones

Approach

Instead of a random train/test split, the models were evaluated with temporal validation: trained on the 2018 World Cup and tested on the 2022 World Cup. This mirrors how a model would actually be used in practice, namely to predict a future tournament.

  • Logistic regression as an interpretable statistical baseline
  • Random forest as a flexible machine learning method, tuned via cross-validated log loss
  • StatsBomb xG as an external industry benchmark

Performance was measured by discrimination (AUC), probabilistic accuracy (Brier score, log loss) and calibration, complemented by classification metrics at the Youden-optimal threshold.

Results

Model AUC Brier score
StatsBomb xG (benchmark) 0.815 0.076
Logistic regression (own model) 0.796 0.081
Random forest (own model) 0.732 0.087

The logistic regression comes within about 0.02 AUC points of StatsBomb's proprietary model, despite being trained on a single tournament. The random forest falls clearly behind on every metric.

Why the simple model wins

  • Limited data: around 1,600 to 1,700 training shots leave little room for a high-variance ensemble to learn non-linear patterns without overfitting.
  • Smooth relationships: the main predictors (distance, angle) relate to scoring probability in a mostly monotone way, which a logit model captures well.
  • Interpretability: the coefficients show directly how distance, pressure or one-on-one situations change the chance of scoring, which makes the model easy to communicate.

The takeaway: more complex is not automatically better. With limited, noisy data, a well-specified statistical model can be both more accurate and more transparent.

Tools

R (StatsBombR, dplyr, purrr, caret, randomForest, pROC), LaTeX.
