Austrian Elections: From Ballots to Coalitions

A complete analysis pipeline for the Austrian parliamentary elections 2019 and 2024: from raw official result files to validated data, seat allocation, coalition maths and a simulation of polling uncertainty. Built entirely in R.

The question

In 2024 the FPÖ won the most votes of any party in either election, the first time a far-right party finished first in a national election in post-war Austria. Yet it did not end up in government. This project shows why: in a proportional system, coalition mathematics matter as much as raw vote shares.

Data pipeline

  • Source: official results of the Federal Ministry of the Interior at municipality, district, state and national level
  • Elections: Nationalratswahl 2019 and 2024, plus the presidential election 2022 as a test case for the validation
  • Automated ingestion: the pipeline detects all result files, harmonises column names and data types, and adapts automatically to new elections, so adding one only requires dropping in the file
  • Validation: six consistency checks, for example that votes add up across geographic levels and that valid plus invalid votes match ballots cast

Seat allocation

Seats were allocated with my own implementation of the D'Hondt method, written in vectorised R without electoral packages.

Coalition modelling

A government needs at least 92 of 183 seats. An exhaustive search over all party combinations shows which coalitions are mathematically possible. In 2024, every two-party majority required the ÖVP or the SPÖ. In the end a three-party coalition of ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS was formed without the FPÖ.

How reliable are polls?

Using the 2024 result as the "true" vote shares, I simulated 100,000 polls with 200 respondents each. Even when the true shares are known exactly, realistic sample sizes produce a wide spread of outcomes, especially for smaller parties near the 4% threshold for entering parliament.

Key takeaways

  • Winning the most votes does not mean winning the most governing power.
  • Polls should be read as distributions, not point predictions.
  • A generalised, validated pipeline turns a one-off analysis into reusable infrastructure.
